The laptop is the factory, the edge is the storefront

Why a capable home machine should build everything and serve nothing.

A laptop with a good graphics chip can draft, summarise, tag, translate and render all day for the price of electricity. It is tempting to go one step further and let it serve the results to the world too. That step is where things go wrong.

Three reasons not to serve from home

It sleeps. A lid closes, a battery runs down, an update restarts it. A public site that disappears when someone goes to lunch is not a site.

It usually can't be reached anyway. Many home connections sit behind the provider's shared address, so inbound connections from the internet never arrive, whatever the router is told.

It shares a connection with people. The same line carries video calls and work. An unattended job that fills it is a bad housemate.

The split

So the work divides cleanly:

Stays on the laptop Goes to the edge
Writing, drafting, bulk text work Finished pages
Building the site Serving them, all day, everywhere
Checking it before it leaves Nothing private, ever

The laptop is a factory: noisy, private, switched off at night. The storefront is a pile of finished files on someone else's always-on network. Static files are cheap to host, hard to break and have no server to attack.

When a page needs to do something

Forms and sign-ups need a little code next to the files. The same rule holds. The storefront takes the order and puts it in a queue; the factory collects the queue when it is awake, does the heavy work, and sends back a result. The laptop only ever dials out. Nothing dials in.

The useful consequence is that the expensive part, the thinking, runs on hardware already paid for, and the part that must never go down is the part that costs almost nothing.
